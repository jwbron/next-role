# Compensation and negotiation preparation

Prepare around the candidate's priorities and real alternatives. Published ranges and salary reports inform questions, they aren't an offer or a promised outcome.

## Before an offer

Ask what matters: recurring cash, location, scope, level, autonomy, stability, hours, benefits, learning, or equity upside. A candidate can decline to record a target or floor. Keep any private threshold out of employer-facing drafts.

Prepare questions about the role's approved range, level, compensation components, and process. Ask the candidate whether they want to state a range, seek the employer's range first, or defer until scope is clearer. Don't supply a number on their behalf. Salary-history rules differ by jurisdiction, don't assume a disclosure is required or lawful to request.

## Establish actual terms

Use written terms where available and record the source/date. Distinguish verbal indications, target compensation, a formal offer, and conditions. Clarify deadline timezone, start date, level, base currency and period, bonus guarantees, signing terms, equity instrument, vesting, benefits, remote policy, on-call, and travel.

Equity questions include issuer, instrument, units, strike price for options, vesting/cliff, exercise window, tax treatment, refresh policy, dilution, sale eligibility, and restrictions. A financing valuation, acquisition, or tender headline doesn't establish employee liquidity.

## Compare like with like

Keep first-year and recurring cash separate. Normalize currency only with an explicit rate and date, don't quietly mix currencies. A target bonus isn't guaranteed. If the guarantee is part of the target bonus, don't add it twice.

Synthetic example: 120,000 annual base, 10,000 guaranteed bonus included in a 15,000 target bonus, and 8,000 first-year signing gives 138,000 first-year guaranteed cash and 143,000 first-year target cash. Recurring guaranteed cash is 130,000 only if the guarantee continues, and recurring target cash is 135,000. A four-year 80,000 grant vesting evenly is 20,000 nominal annual equity, not 80,000 per year or guaranteed proceeds.

Model actual vesting by year where it isn't even. Treat private equity as scenarios, not cash. Identify excluded taxes, exercise costs, benefit values, and risk. A gross comparison isn't take-home pay.

## Model private equity

Every number in this section is hypothetical and only illustrates the arithmetic. Don't treat these figures as market data.

### Value the grant two ways

A recruiter's equity figure is often the share count times the preferred price from the last financing round. That price applies to investors' preferred shares, which carry rights common shares don't. The 409A valuation is an independent appraisal of common stock, usually well below preferred, and it sets the option strike price.

For options, the candidate gains only the spread: the future common price minus the strike, times the shares. Synthetic example: 20,000 options, a strike and 409A of 3.00, and a preferred price of 10.00. The headline figure is 200,000 (20,000 times 10.00). The spread at the preferred price is 140,000 (20,000 times 7.00). At the 409A price the spread is zero, because the strike equals it. Exercising also costs cash, 60,000 here, plus any taxes.

RSUs have no strike, so 20,000 RSUs at the same preferred price show 200,000 nominal value. Private-company RSUs often vest only after both a time condition and a liquidity event, so they may pay nothing until one happens. Show both the preferred-price and 409A views and say which one any figure uses.

### Dilution and liquidation preferences

Later financing rounds issue new shares and shrink the candidate's percentage. Synthetic example: two later rounds that each add 15% dilution leave 72.25% of the original percentage (0.85 times 0.85). Model at least one diluted case.

Preferred shares usually get their money back before common shareholders receive anything. With a 1x non-participating preference, investors take the larger of their investment or their converted share. Preferences matter most when the exit is below or near the total amount raised. Synthetic example: a company that raised 200 million and sells for 150 million may return nothing to common. At a much larger exit, preferred converts and the preference matters little. Ask about the preference stack when plausible exits are modest.

### Exit scenarios with explicit weights

Write each scenario with an exit type, a per-share common value after dilution and preferences, a probability, and timing. Include a case where nothing becomes sellable within the horizon. The probabilities are the candidate's judgment, not a forecast, and they must add to 100%.

| Scenario | Common value per share | Spread on 20,000 options | Probability | Weighted value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No liquidity within four years, or failure | 0 | 0 | 50% | 0 |
| Small sale, preferences take most of it | 2.00 | 0 (below strike) | 20% | 0 |
| Moderate outcome | 12.00 | 180,000 | 20% | 36,000 |
| Strong outcome | 30.00 | 540,000 | 10% | 54,000 |

The probability-weighted four-year value in this synthetic example is 90,000 before exercise costs and taxes, against a 200,000 headline. Adjust the weights with the candidate and show how sensitive the result is. Don't present one weighted number as the answer.

### Liquidity timing

- Tender offers: the company or an investor buys some employee shares. They're occasional, may cap amounts or exclude recent hires, and a past tender doesn't promise another.
- Acquisition: proceeds depend on deal terms and preferences. Unvested equity may be assumed, converted, accelerated, or cancelled.
- IPO: shares usually can't be sold during a lockup period after listing, often several months.

### Separate near-term cash from expected value

Show two views side by side. A two-year cash view counts base, guaranteed bonus, and sign-on that will actually be paid, and counts equity only when a confirmed sale path exists in that window. A four-year view adds the probability-weighted equity value. A candidate with near-term cash needs may weigh the first view more heavily, and that's a legitimate choice.

### Estimate when only an option count is known

Sometimes the candidate only knows "20,000 options." An approximate share count or price can be inferred, and every result should be labeled as an estimate:

- From a peer's grant: if someone at a similar level was told their grant's percentage, their share count divided by that percentage estimates fully diluted shares.
- From typical grant percentages by stage and level in a dated survey: if a survey suggested 0.1% for this stage and level, 20,000 options would imply about 20 million fully diluted shares.
- From the last valuation: post-money valuation divided by estimated fully diluted shares approximates the preferred price. A synthetic 500 million valuation over 50 million shares gives about 10.00.

Cross-check the estimates against each other, record the source and date of each input, and replace them with the company's numbers as soon as possible.

### Questions that replace estimates

- How many shares or units, and which instrument: ISO, NSO, or RSU?
- What's the strike price and the current 409A, and when was the 409A set?
- What was the preferred price in the latest round, and how many fully diluted shares are outstanding?
- What's the vesting schedule and cliff?
- How long is the post-departure exercise window?
- Is early exercise allowed?
- What's the refresh grant policy?
- Have there been tender offers, who was eligible, and on what terms?
- Is there a sign-on payment, and what's the clawback?

Tax treatment of ISOs, NSOs, and early exercise varies by jurisdiction and situation. Flag it for qualified tax advice.

### Leveling is the main lever

At companies that set compensation by level, each level's band is usually fixed, and the largest change comes from the level itself. If the candidate's scope and evidence support a higher level, ask about leveling before negotiating within a band.

## Make an ask

Clarify missing material terms before countering. Prioritize a few requests and their reasons, then identify acceptable alternatives. Consider level, scope, start date, signing payment, review timing, time off, remote terms, and other non-pay needs alongside base salary.

Draft a concise expression of interest and a concrete request. Use genuine competing options and actual deadlines only with permission. Never invent an offer, bluff a deadline, or imply willingness to walk away that the candidate hasn't confirmed. Don't send the draft.

## Decide

Compare the work and package against the candidate's own criteria. Show what could change the recommendation. Request final written terms after an agreed change and check contingencies before treating it as settled. Flag legal, immigration, and tax questions for qualified advice. The candidate makes acceptance, rejection, resignation, and disclosure decisions.
