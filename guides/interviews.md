# Prepare for the actual interview

The goal is a useful conversation and a better candidate decision, not a memorized performance. The invitation and recruiting contact establish format. Public interview reports and predicted topics are uncertain practice inputs.

## Build the brief

Record stage, date, timezone, duration, participants, format, allowed tools, source, and unknowns. If details aren't confirmed, label the brief provisional. Ask how much preparation time is available and what the candidate most wants to improve.

Select an introduction, two or three confirmed stories, questions that affect the candidate's decision, and the highest-priority gap. Put deeper reference material below the quick brief. Prefer a short actionable document over an exhaustive topic dump.

## Match the stage

| Stage | Prepare | Questions worth asking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recruiter | Accurate background, motivation, location, timing, role scope | Level, interview format, pay range, eligibility, working hours |
| Hiring manager | Ownership, collaboration, tradeoffs, adoption, outcomes | First problems to solve, team support, success criteria, on-call |
| Behavioral | Specific conflict, feedback, failure, ambiguity, leadership stories | How decisions and disagreements work in practice |
| Coding or debugging | Clarification, implementation, tests, diagnosis | Allowed stack, environment, tools, expected demonstration |
| Systems design | Requirements, state, interfaces, failure behavior, tradeoffs | Scale assumptions, priorities, data constraints |
| Product, design, data, or portfolio | Relevant work walkthrough, user evidence, options, evaluation | Audience, deliverable, dataset access, decision criteria |
| Staff or leadership | Cross-team direction, influence, scope, mentoring, delivery | Decision authority, alignment, support, resourcing |
| Final or executive | Consistent motivation, unresolved concerns, candidate decision criteria | Scope, business direction, sustainable workload, expectations |
| Take-home | Instructions, deadline, time budget, allowed assistance, delivery rules | Ambiguities and what to omit |

Don't infer an interviewer's personality, private views, or hiring rubric. Public professional background may help choose topics, but doesn't establish what they will ask.

## Build practice from evidence

Choose one core exercise tied to the role's work, one variation that tests adaptation, and one observed gap. Label every invented problem as original practice. Don't present guessed prompts as leaked or recovered employer questions.

For a story, ask follow-ups about the candidate's decision, alternatives, collaboration, and evidence. For technical work, ask how a requirement change affects the solution. For portfolio work, ask what was tested, what the candidate owned, and what they'd change.

## Rehearse and improve

During a mock, ask one question at a time and wait. Don't supply the model answer first. After the answer, ask one useful follow-up, then offer evidence-based feedback when requested or at the agreed checkpoint. Separate content gaps, communication suggestions, and factual uncertainties.

End with two or three specific changes to practice, not a fabricated pass probability. Save feedback only with the candidate's agreement. A mock isn't an actual interview and never changes employer-process status.

## Before the event

Check timezone, meeting access, accessibility needs the candidate chooses to discuss, permitted tools, environment, and screen sharing. Have a low-tech fallback for diagrams or tool failure. Never provide covert live assistance against the interview's rules.
